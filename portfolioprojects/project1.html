Mosaic | Media-Tracking Prototype

One app for everything worth reading, watching, playing, and hearing.

Project Overview

Mosaic is a mobile app that unifies media tracking into a single platform. Built over a 10-week capstone with a four-person team, the project moved through competitive analysis, user interviews, and two rounds of usability testing, from a low-fidelity prototype to a high-fidelity design.

The Challenge

Tracking media means juggling apps, or tracking nothing. Mosaic brings it all into one place.

Entertainment media spans books, movies, TV, music, and games, but tracking it doesn't. Most people juggle separate apps for each format, or give up tracking altogether. Mosaic explores what a single, unified platform for documenting, organizing, and reflecting on media could look like.

Goals

Three goals shaped Mosaic's design: effortless tracking across media types, genuine support for reflection, and simple collection-building. Rather than separate tools for separate formats, Mosaic gives users one place to track, rate, and review everything they consume. We measured success through ease-of-use ratings, satisfaction scores, and task completion rates.

Research

Feature Matrix

My team built a feature matrix comparing Mosaic's planned functionality against three competitors: Letterboxd, Goodreads, and Metacritic. Each existing tool covered only one or two media types, and none supported visually driven, flexible collection-building. This gap directly shaped Mosaic's multi-format tracking and shelf features.

Heuristic Analysis

I conducted a heuristic evaluation of Letterboxd and Goodreads using Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics, logging every violation and rating its severity. Letterboxd's most frequent issues were inconsistent visual standards and unclear system feedback; Goodreads struggled most with user control, including a lack of undo options when navigating between screens. These findings helped us avoid repeating the same friction points in our own design.

Literature Review

We reviewed relevant literature to provide insights that informed the design.

Virtual possessions are valued through long term reflection, personal organization, and selective expressions of identity.

“Virtual Possessions” by William Odom, John Zimmerman, and Jodi Forlizzi (2010).

Collaborative media tools can support connection, emotional expression, and discovery.

“Hitting Pause: How User Perceptions of Collaborative Playlists Evolved in the United States During the COVID-19 Pandemic” by So Yeon Park, Emily Redmond, Jonathan Berger, and Blair Kaneshiro (2022).

Personal data apps should make it easy to track your habits and turn that information into useful insights.

“A Stage-Based Model of Personal Informatics Systems” by Ian Li, Anind Dey, and Jodi Forlizzi (2010).

Interviews

My team conducted semi-structured interviews with three participants to understand current media-tracking habits, pain points, and openness to a new solution.

Key Questions

  • How do people currently track (or not track) the media they consume?
  • What role do lists and organization play in their habits?
  • How important is reflection, and what stops people from doing it?
  • How do friends and social platforms influence discovery?
  • What would an ideal tracking solution look like?

What We Found

Interviews revealed that most people default to passive tracking, like leaving a finished book on a shelf, rather than actively logging anything. All three participants said they wanted to write reflections but rarely followed through, citing effort and low motivation as the main obstacles. Friends and social platforms, especially Instagram, played a larger role in discovery than aggregated reviews. Participants were also confident, experienced list-makers, just not for media specifically.

Research → Design: These findings shaped three design principles: keep every interaction as low-effort as possible, build a lightweight social layer around trusted recommendations, and lean into shelves and lists as a familiar, low-friction entry point into the app.

Low Fidelity Prototype

Our first prototype focused on structure, navigation, and flow rather than visual design. It covered three core paths: onboarding, logging a diary entry, and adding an item to a shelf.

Usability Testing Round 1

Scenarios

My team and I conducted moderated usability tests with 5 users. They were presented with 3 scenarios; go through onboarding, log a reflection for a recently watched movie, and explore a shelf’s view and add a new item to it.

  • Scenario 1: Imagine you have just downloaded Mosaic for the very first time. Please proceed through the onboarding process in the way that feels most natural to you.
  • Scenario 2: Imagine you recently watched the movie Project Hail Mary and have just opened the app to write a reflection on it. Use the prototype to log a new entry in the way that feels most natural to you.
  • Scenario 3: Imagine you want to add Dune: Part One to your Sci-Fi list. Use the prototype in the way that feels most natural to you and add a new movie to your previously made list.

Metrics Tracked

  • Post-onboarding mental model accuracy
  • Task completion
  • Perceived task length
  • Open-ended feedback on confusion points

Success Criteria

Participants should leave onboarding with an accurate sense of Mosaic's purpose, locate the correct entry point for logging media without assistance, and rate the shelf-adding process as easy to understand.

Results

Onboarding failed to establish a clear mental model. All participants struggled to explain Mosaic's purpose afterward. The “+” icon in the navigation menu was a major source of confusion, with most participants instinctively heading to the Diary page instead to log an entry. The search step for adding a new entry also caused repeated backtracking. Despite this, participants responded well to task pacing and rated the shelves page highly for clarity.

What we changed: Based on Round 1 findings, we redesigned onboarding to include explanatory screens before sign-up, removed the “+” icon from the navigation menu in favor of a dedicated entry point on the Diary page, and improved the visibility of the shelf view toggle. The prototype was also fully visually designed for this round.

Usability Testing Round 2

Scenarios

Five new participants completed four tasks: (1) onboarding, (2) logging a reflection, (3) exploring shelf views and adding an item, and (4) creating a new shelf.

Metrics Tracked

  • Post-onboarding confidence (5-point Likert scale)
  • Task success type (direct, indirect, or assisted)
  • Task-specific confidence and demand ratings
  • System Usability Scale (SUS) score

Success Criteria

The participants should leave onboarding with high confidence in Mosaic's purpose, complete all four tasks without needing significant help, and report low perceived demand for regular use.

Results

94.5 Mean SUS score, rated “A+” across all five participants

All participants left onboarding with a strong understanding of Mosaic's purpose, a clear improvement over Round 1. The updated entry point for logging media reduced friction significantly, though some participants still looked for it in the wrong place first. The redesigned shelf view toggle fully resolved the earlier discoverability issue. The team recorded a mean SUS score of 94.5, rated “A+” across all five participants. The clearest remaining issue was the preference-selection step during onboarding, which several participants found unnecessary and mildly frustrating.

Closing / Future Work

Mosaic validated a real need for unified, low-effort media tracking, and testing showed major usability gains between the low- and high-fidelity rounds. But the project also surfaced a challenge that design alone can't solve: even with an easy-to-use interface, users still lack intrinsic motivation to write reflections. Future work should explore how to lower the perceived cost of reflection, whether through more lightweight formats, prompts, or incentives, alongside continued refinement of onboarding and entry-point discoverability. There's also room to explore deeper integration with external platforms, a feature multiple participants requested directly.

The Problem

Tracking media means juggling a separate app for each format, or tracking nothing at all.

Books, movies, TV, music and games each have their own tracker, and none of them support flexible, visual collections. In interviews, people said they mostly track passively, like leaving a finished book on a shelf. They wanted to write reflections but rarely did, because it felt like too much effort. They also trusted friends’ recommendations more than aggregated reviews.

The Solution

Mosaic is one mobile app for tracking, rating and reflecting on every type of media. Three principles from the research shaped it:

  • Low effort everywhere. Logging an entry or a reflection takes as few steps as possible.
  • Shelves and lists first. People already make lists, so collections are the easy way in.
  • A light social layer. Discovery is built around recommendations from people you trust.

After the first round of testing, onboarding was rebuilt to explain the app before sign-up, logging moved to a dedicated entry point on the Diary page, and the shelf view toggle was made easier to find.

The Outcome

94.5 Mean SUS score in Round 2, rated “A+” across all five participants

In Round 1, every participant struggled to explain what Mosaic was for after onboarding. In Round 2, all of them understood it. The open question is motivation: even with an easy interface, people still skip writing reflections, so future work should make reflecting feel lighter.
